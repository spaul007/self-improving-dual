Beliefs as Scored Hypotheses

What the belief layer is doing, seen as an optimization problem: a probabilistic model over edit strategies that is forced to make falsifiable predictions, pays a proper scoring loss when the judge rules, and rewrites both itself and the instructions it was written under.

1. Three optimizations, nested

The system optimizes three different objects at three different time scales. Keeping them apart is the key to reading any result.

2. A belief is a falsifiable bet

A belief is not a sentence about the world; it is a scoped probability. Each one names a strategy from the run's registry (optionally narrowed by an area), a kind, and a number in [0.05, 0.95]:

two kinds of belief, two conditional probabilities implementation belief: p = P( implementation is sound | strategy s, area a ) strategy belief: p = P( judge says "improved" | strategy s, area a, implementation sound )

The split is the credit-assignment device. A failed edit can fail because the idea was wrong or because the code did not do what it claimed; conflating them poisons every conclusion about the idea. So the strategy belief is conditioned on soundness, and it is simply not scored when the implementation was unsound — an unsound attempt says nothing about the strategy.

The bet is placed before the outcome exists: when a node is recorded, the code looks up the most specific matching belief per kind and freezes its p. The belief writer never gets to predict after seeing the result, and it cannot decline to predict — a measured node no belief covers is scored at p = 0.5, a loss of 0.25. Silence costs exactly as much as a coin flip, so the document is pushed toward coverage and commitment. The one exception is the first node of a strategy nobody had tried, which is skipped rather than charged: no belief could have existed.

3. Why the label is a judge, not the score

The obvious label — did the node's score go up? — is nearly unusable at this batch size. A 16-case batch carries a standard error near ±0.10, so a Δ of +0.08 is one sigma of noise; the run's two "best" nodes both fell by 0.03 on their second batch. Training probabilities against that would train them to predict noise.

The judge is an LLM pass that reads what the score cannot: the edit's implementation, the fire counts of the instrumentation the editor was told to add (trace.log at every decision point), and the per-check results on the node's own cases. It answers narrower, more stable questions — did this mechanism run? did it change the checks it targeted? did it break anything? — and grades its own evidence (strong / moderate / weak). Weak verdicts stay pending; unclear ones are never scored. The score is shown to the belief writer only as context, with its standard error attached.

What this buys. A verifier that never fires, or that fires on the wrong condition, is invisible to the score (the node just looks average) but explicit to the judge ("dead verifier"). In the live run, that single distinction is why the memory arm stopped writing verification code and kept writing prompt rules, while the arm without beliefs kept stacking verifiers it could not tell were dead.

What it costs. The judge is itself a model; its verdict can be wrong, and the beliefs inherit that error. The calibration report carries a "judge vs. score" diagnostic for exactly this reason — cases where the two disagree are where the labels are least trustworthy.

4. The scoring rule and what it makes the writer do

Brier loss per scored prediction; 0.25 is the uninformative reference L(p, y) = (p − y)² y = 1 if the judge's verdict confirms the kind, else 0 uncovered kind: p := 0.5 → L = 0.25 track line per belief: n · mean L · outcomes (which nodes said yes / no)

Brier is a strictly proper scoring rule: the expected loss is minimized only by reporting the probability you actually hold, so hedging toward 0.5 is never free and overconfidence is punished quadratically. The writer sees its loss two ways — a machine-written track line under every belief, regenerated after each scoring batch, and a calibration report in its prompt listing the worst misses, the uncovered nodes it silently paid for, and any citation it misquoted against the records. A belief that keeps missing is expected to be revised or defended, and either move is visible in the archive.

The update itself is not a Bayesian computation. It is a language model rewriting the whole document under a contract — fixed sections, registry-valid scopes, cited evidence that the code verifies, hard caps — with the losses in front of it. What is guaranteed is the accounting: every number is frozen before it can be gamed, every citation is checked, and every version is archived. What is not guaranteed is that the writer responds to its losses optimally; that is what the third loop is for.

5. Optimizing the optimizer

The belief writer works from a guidance text: how to scope beliefs, how confident to be, what to treat as evidence. That text is itself a variable. Every eight scored predictions, one model call is shown the misses since the last revision — each with the belief text that made the prediction, the judge's verdict and reason, and the score in context — and asked for a critique of what went wrong systematically (hedging everything at 0.5, ignoring instrumentation evidence, scoping too broadly) and a revised guidance text.

Because every prediction is stamped with the guidance version it was made under, versions can be compared on their own mean loss. Before each step the optimizer checks for regressions: if the current version, with enough samples, is worse than an earlier one by more than a margin, the earlier text is restored. This is hill-climbing with a memory, on a very noisy objective, with very few samples — in the live run the first revision told the writer to predict the verdict rather than the score and to cover every edit type with a committed probability; the second was rolled back and re-attempted. Expect it to move slowly.

6. How beliefs act on the search

Beliefs never touch the search policy. The bandit chooses which node to expand and evaluate on scores alone; the belief document is advice the editor reads before it edits — which strategies are sound, which are saturating, which node to look at for a working implementation, what to try next. Its influence is only as strong as its evidence, and it can be ignored.

This is deliberate. The control arm of the experiment differs from the memory arm in exactly one thing: whether this curated layer exists. Both agents read the same raw evidence (parent traces, case results, the code of any node in the run); only one also reads a calibrated summary of what has been learned. The question the experiment asks is therefore not "does memory help?" but "does a scored, judge-labelled summary beat raw access to the same history?"

7. What to measure, and what would falsify it

  • Calibration: mean Brier over time, per belief and per guidance version. Below 0.25 the beliefs carry information; converging toward it means they have degraded to coin flips. The live run sits at 0.19–0.24 after 20 predictions.
  • Coverage: the share of measured nodes scored at p = 0.5. High coverage with poor calibration is confident nonsense; low coverage with good calibration is a model that only bets on easy cases.
  • Judge–score agreement: where the judge's "improved" coincides with positive well-measured Δ. Persistent disagreement means the label is drifting from the objective.
  • Behavioural effect: what the editor does after reading the beliefs — the kind and size of edits, whether cited nodes are actually opened — visible in the session transcripts.
  • The outcome that matters: the final selected node's full-train score against the control arm's, over more than one seed.

The hypothesis fails if the beliefs stay uninformative (Brier ≈ 0.25), if the guidance optimizer cannot improve them, or if a well-calibrated document still produces no better harness than raw history does. Each of those is distinguishable from the artifacts the layer leaves behind.

8. Known weaknesses

  • Sample sizes are tiny: tens of scored predictions per run, and rollback decisions on eight. Noise dominates early.
  • The judge is an LLM grading another LLM's code from traces; its verdicts are stable questions but not ground truth.
  • Beliefs are scoped by registry tags assigned by a tagger model; a mis-tag mismatches a prediction to the wrong belief.
  • Exploitation pressure: the document rewards what has been judged sound, and nothing in it yet prices the value of trying an untried mechanism.
  • The update is a rewrite by a model, not an inference rule; two runs with identical losses can produce different documents.
